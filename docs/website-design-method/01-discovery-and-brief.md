# Phase 1 — Discovery & Brief

**Question this phase answers:** _What is this, who is it for, and what must it
accomplish in this version?_

**Artifact produced:** a written brief.

**Exit criteria:** a stranger could read the brief and (a) correctly describe
what you do, (b) name the primary audience, and (c) state the one action the
site is built to produce. If they can't, you are not done.

---

## Why this phase exists

Every avoidable disaster in web design is a Phase 1 failure wearing a Phase 3
costume. "We can't agree on the colors" almost always means "we never agreed on
who this is for." The brief is cheap to change and expensive to skip. It is the
document every later decision gets checked against.

The Civic Blueprint brief opened by refusing the obvious goal. It did **not**
say "launch a public platform." It said: build _"the smallest credible website
that helps Civic Blueprint move from internal document development to external
challenge."_ That single act of narrowing — smallest, credible, one specific job
— shaped everything downstream. Your brief should do the same kind of narrowing.

## The principle: define the version, not the dream

A brief is for **this version**, for **the first right audience**. The dream is a
distraction at this stage. Two moves make a brief useful:

1. **Narrow the audience to the first right one.** Not "everyone." The person
   whose reaction will teach you the most, soonest.
2. **Name what you are deferring.** A list of "explicitly not now" items is what
   lets you ship. Without it, every good idea is an obligation.

## The questions to ask

Ask these roughly in order. The early answers constrain the later ones.

### Identity

- In one sentence, what is this and what does it do? (Ban "innovative,"
  "leading," "dynamic," "solutions." If you need them, you don't yet know the
  answer.)
- Why does it exist? What's broken, missing, or possible that this addresses?
- What's the honest current state — early/provisional, mature, mid-pivot? (This
  sets how confident the copy is allowed to sound.)

### Audience

- Who is the **first right** visitor? Describe a real human in a real situation,
  not a demographic.
- What do they already believe when they arrive? What would make them bounce?
- Who is the secondary audience?
- Who is this **explicitly not for**? (This question does more design work than
  any other.)

### Purpose

- If a visitor does exactly one thing, what is it? (The "single action.")
- What does the visitor need to believe before they'll take that action?
- What's the smallest proof that you can deliver on your claim?

### Posture & tone

- Finish: "When someone lands here, it should feel like ____."
- List 3–5 things it should feel like, and 3–5 things it must **not**.
- Is the right register "campaign" (build belief/loyalty) or "invitation"
  (invite scrutiny/trust)? Most serious projects want the second and reach for
  the first by accident.

### Scope & success

- What ships in v1?
- What are you explicitly deferring? (Write the list. Defend it.)
- How will you know v1 worked? Lead with qualitative signals before metrics.

## Turning answers into a brief

Group the answers under these headings. This is the structure the Civic
Blueprint Phase 1 brief used, lightly generalized:

1. **Purpose** — the narrowed goal for this version, in two sentences.
2. **What this phase is for / not for** — two bulleted lists. The "not for" list
   is mandatory.
3. **Primary / secondary / non-audience.**
4. **Product principles** — 4–6 durable rules (see below).
5. **Core message** — the 3–5 ideas the homepage must land quickly.
6. **Information architecture** — the minimum set of pages.
7. **Success criteria** — qualitative first, quantitative second.
8. **Technical guidance** — selection criteria for the stack, not the stack
   itself (that's Phase 5).
9. **Explicit deferrals** — what later phases may add.

### Product principles worth stealing

Civic Blueprint's five Phase 1 product principles generalize well. Adapt the
wording, keep the spirit:

1. **Plain language at the entry point** — explain it without jargon up top.
2. **Scannable writing** — headings, short paragraphs, conclusion-first; people
   scan before they read.
3. **Objective tone** — sound like a credible source, not a campaign.
4. **Accessibility from the start** — semantic HTML, contrast, keyboard, mobile,
   alt text. Built in, never retrofitted.
5. **Challenge, not applause** (their domain-specific one) — the equivalent for
   you is whatever posture your single action implies. For a consultancy it
   might be "credibility, not hype." Name yours.

## Common traps

- **The everyone trap.** "It's for everyone" produces a site for no one. Pick
  the first right audience.
- **The feature inventory.** Listing everything you _can_ do instead of the one
  thing a visitor should do. The old Baker Street site is a textbook case: digital
  agency _and_ technology partner _and_ architecture _and_ blockchain, so a
  visitor couldn't tell what to hire them for.
- **Borrowing someone else's brief.** Competitor sites are evidence, not
  templates. Their brief answered their questions, not yours.
- **Skipping the deferral list.** Without it, v1 never ships.

## What "done" looks like

You have a brief document with the nine sections above. The "not for" and
"explicitly deferred" lists are non-empty. You can state the single action in
one breath. Now — and only now — go to
[Phase 2: UX & Architecture](./02-ux-and-architecture.md).
