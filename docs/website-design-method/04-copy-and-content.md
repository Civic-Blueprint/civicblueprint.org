# Phase 4 — Copy & Content

**Question this phase answers:** _What are the exact words, in what voice, with
what honesty — for every section the UX defined?_

**Artifact produced:** a copy draft, section by section, ready to paste into the
build.

**Exit criteria:** every section from the Phase 2 flow has real headline + body
copy (not Lorem, not placeholders), plus meta text and any provenance lines.

---

## Why this phase exists

Layout is the skeleton; copy is the thing a visitor actually consumes. Most of
what people call "the website" is words. Doing copy as its own phase — after the
structure is fixed but before the build — means you write to a known shape and
the developer transcribes instead of inventing. It also catches the moment when
a beautiful section turns out to have nothing to say.

## Principle: copy is the argument, finally spoken

Phases 1–3 found and shaped the argument. Now you say it out loud. The copy
inherits its job from the funnel: the hero serves Layer 1, the context strip and
core claim serve Layer 2, the featured proof serves Layer 3. If a sentence
doesn't move the visitor along that path, it's ornament.

## Voice rules that traveled well

Civic Blueprint's content guidelines generalize to almost any serious site:

- **Lead with the conclusion.** Don't warm up. State the point, then support it.
- **Short sections over long blocks.** People scan; reward scanning with
  meaningful headings and tight paragraphs.
- **Concrete over conceptual.** "Housing is scarce where it should be abundant"
  beats "suboptimal market outcomes." Name real things.
- **No promotional copy.** Hype lowers credibility for a serious audience. Sound
  like a trustworthy source, not a campaign. (For a commercial site, the
  equivalent is "specific over superlative" — "we shipped X for Y in Z weeks"
  beats "world-class solutions.")
- **Outbound links increase credibility** where they let a reader verify a
  claim.
- **Tell the visitor exactly what you want.** Vague asks get vague responses.

Write your own 3–5 voice rules before drafting, so the draft has a standard to
meet.

## How to write each section

Go section by section through the Phase 2 flow. For each:

1. **State its one job** (from Phase 2) at the top of the draft.
2. **Write the headline as the strongest true sentence** that does that job. The
   headline is the section; the body supports it.
3. **Write the body to the depth layer it serves.** Intuitive sections stay
   plain; technical sections may go dense. Same idea, right depth.
4. **End with the action or the link** the section is meant to produce.

### The hero gets written twice

Draft the hero, ship it, then come back and cut it by a third. The hero is the
most-read and most-over-written part of any site. Civic Blueprint's hero earns
its length only because every clause does work: a question headline ("What if
we're not as divided as we think?"), a subhead that states the finding, a
posture line that pre-empts the skeptic, and three CTAs in priority order.

### The featured-proof copy is a mini product page

Your strongest evidence deserves real copy: a title, a short "why this matters,"
the specific questions or results it addresses, and an honest "here's where it
might be wrong / here's the catch." Civic Blueprint's memo panel even lists "Key
test questions" and an explicit "if this adds nothing, we need to know." That
honesty is what makes proof persuasive.

## Provenance and honesty

Decide how the site tells the truth about itself. Two levers:

1. **Honesty lines in the copy.** Civic Blueprint keeps lines like _"a working
   thesis backed by evidence, not a settled claim."_ For a business, the
   equivalent is being specific about what you do and don't do. Honesty reads as
   confidence.
2. **Provenance labels**, if you publish AI-assisted or collaboratively authored
   content. This repo uses a four-value standard — `human`, `collaborative`,
   `ai-generated, steward-curated`, `ai-generated` — surfaced on the page (see
   the hero's "content provenance" link and the memo panel's `collaborative`
   pill). If your project makes claims people will act on, labeling who/what
   produced them is a trust multiplier, not a confession.

## Meta and social copy

Don't leave this to the build. Write:

- **Page title** (<~60 chars) — Civic Blueprint's is the homepage's actual
  question: _"Civic Blueprint | What if we already agree on where we're going?"_
- **Meta description** (~155 chars) — a scannable, accurate summary.
- **OG/Twitter title + description** — can be punchier than the meta; this is
  the share-card voice.

These are copy, and they're the first words most people read — often before they
ever reach the hero.

## The questions to ask

- What are this site's 3–5 voice rules?
- For each section: what's the single strongest true headline?
- Where can a concrete example replace an abstraction?
- What's the honest "here's where this might be wrong / here's the catch"?
- Exactly what are you asking the visitor to do, in their words?
- What honesty lines should the site carry?
- Do we need provenance labels, and where do they appear?
- What are the title, meta description, and share-card copy?

## Common traps

- **Lorem ipsum that survives to launch.** Placeholder copy hides the fact that
  a section has no point. Write real words early.
- **Warming up.** Three sentences before the actual claim. Cut to the claim.
- **Superlatives instead of specifics.** "Leading, innovative, world-class"
  says nothing. Numbers, names, and dates say something.
- **Writing for yourself.** Insider vocabulary the first-right audience doesn't
  share. Read it as them.
- **Forgetting meta/social copy** until the build, where it gets auto-generated
  and bland.

## What "done" looks like

Every section has real copy at the right depth, the hero has been cut once, the
proof reads like a mini product page, honesty/provenance is decided, and meta +
social copy exist. Proceed to
[Phase 5: Build & Validation](./05-build-and-validation.md).
