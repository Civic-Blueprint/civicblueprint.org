# Phase 6 — Repositioning Without Rebuild

**Question this phase answers:** _The business, thesis, or offering changed. What
do we keep, what do we reframe, and what do we actually replace?_

**Artifact produced:** a reframe brief (a short, focused update — not a new
brief from scratch).

**Use this phase when:** the offering pivots, the audience shifts, or the core
message evolves — and you're tempted to "redo the whole site." Don't. Reframe
it.

---

## Why this phase exists

This is the phase that pays back the discipline of the first five. Because every
decision was traceable to an answer, a change in the _argument_ does not require
a change in the _system_. You can swap the thesis and keep the type, color,
components, infrastructure, and most of the structure. The rebuild instinct —
throw it all out, start fresh — is almost always wrong and almost always
expensive.

This is the single most relevant phase for an existing business doing an
offering pivot, because it tells you exactly how little you have to discard.

## The proof this works: Civic Blueprint's Phase 1 → Phase 2

Civic Blueprint executed a genuine repositioning and changed remarkably little
of the machine:

- **Phase 1 narrative:** problem → principles → proposals. ("Here's what's
  broken. Here's a framework. Challenge it.")
- **Phase 2 narrative:** shared aspiration → alignment evidence → principles as
  outcome targets → proposals as realignment. ("What if we already agree on
  where we're going? Here's the evidence. Here's the gap.")

That is a real shift in the central claim. Yet the Phase 2 brief explicitly kept:
the visual identity and design system, the component architecture (panels,
cards, grids, corner marks, typography), the challenge-oriented tone, the
proof-of-usefulness artifact, the contribution paths, the epistemic posture, and
the entire technical stack. What changed was the **homepage narrative arc** and a
**new evidence section** — framing and one addition, not a rebuild.

The same hero component, the same `.blueprint-panel`, the same Public
Sans/Source Serif pairing now carried a different argument. That's the goal.

## The principle: keep / reframe / replace

Sort everything the site contains into three buckets. Be honest and lean heavily
toward the first two.

### Keep (almost everything structural)

The design system, components, infrastructure, contribution/conversion
mechanics, and validation setup are **argument-independent**. They were built to
carry _an_ argument, not _that_ argument. Keep them unless the new positioning
literally contradicts them (it rarely does).

### Reframe (most of the content)

Most existing content is still true; it just needs new framing. Civic
Blueprint's principles didn't change a word — they were re-introduced as "shared
outcome targets" instead of "our values." Reframing is usually:

- new hero and headline (the new argument's strongest sentence)
- new or rewritten intro/transition copy that re-contextualizes existing
  sections
- updated section order to match the new narrative arc
- updated meta/social copy

### Replace (the few sections that no longer fit)

Some sections were load-bearing for the old argument and are dead weight for the
new one. Replace or retire them, and add the one or two genuinely new sections
the new thesis requires (Civic Blueprint added "The Finding" and "The Gap" and an
evidence page). Adding a section is cheap when the design system already exists.

## How to run a reposition

1. **Re-answer only the changed Phase 1 questions.** Usually: the one-sentence
   truth, the single action, and possibly the primary audience. Most of the
   brief still stands.
2. **Redraw the narrative arc (Phase 2).** Old arc → new arc, in one line. Then
   reorder/rename sections to fit it. The IA and component vocabulary usually
   survive intact.
3. **Do NOT reopen Phase 3** unless the new feeling words genuinely conflict
   with the anchor. A pivot in _offering_ rarely means a pivot in _identity_.
   (If the brand mark itself is changing, that's a different, larger project —
   treat it as a new anchor and re-run Phase 3 deliberately.)
4. **Rewrite the copy that carries the new argument (Phase 4).** Hero first, then
   transitions, then the new sections. Reuse everything that's still true.
5. **Preserve the old version.** Archive the previous site at a stable URL before
   the new framing ships, so you can compare and roll back. (Civic Blueprint's
   plan archived Phase 1 at a public URL before Phase 2 went live.)
6. **Re-validate (Phase 5).** Same gates: build passes, snapshots updated
   intentionally, a11y holds, a real person completes the (possibly new) single
   action.

## When a rebuild IS justified

Be honest about the exceptions. Start over only if:

- the **anchor itself** changed (new name, new logo, new brand identity), or
- the **content shape** changed so fundamentally that the stack no longer fits
  (e.g., a reading site becoming a real-time data app), or
- the original wasn't built with this method and has no traceable decisions to
  preserve.

A change in _what you sell_ is almost never one of these. A change in _who you
are_ might be.

## The questions to ask

- What is the new thesis/offering in one sentence?
- Which Phase 1 answers actually changed, and which only feel like they did?
- Old narrative arc → new narrative arc, in one line?
- What can be reframed (kept content, new framing) vs. genuinely replaced?
- What one or two new sections does the new thesis require?
- Does the visual anchor still hold, or did the brand itself change?
- Where will the old version be archived before the new one ships?

## Common traps

- **Rebuild reflex.** Treating a message change as a reason to discard a working
  system. It's the most expensive way to make a small change.
- **Identity drift by accident.** Quietly redoing colors/type during a content
  pivot, so the site loses the coherence it earned. Change identity only on
  purpose.
- **Reframe that's really a contradiction.** If the new copy fights the design
  system at every turn, the anchor may actually have changed — stop and re-run
  Phase 3 deliberately instead of forcing it.
- **No archive.** Losing the ability to compare or revert.

## What "done" looks like

You have a short reframe brief, a new narrative arc, a clear keep/reframe/replace
list, rewritten argument-carrying copy, an archived previous version, and a
re-validated site — having kept the design system and infrastructure you already
built.
