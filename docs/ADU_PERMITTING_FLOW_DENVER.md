# Adding an ADU (accessory dwelling unit) in Denver, Colorado

The actual approvals you need, in the order they actually happen.

This is a working one-pager about how permitting for a backyard or basement accessory dwelling unit actually flows in one real city — Denver. It is the housing sibling of the [childcare-licensing flow](CHILDCARE_LICENSING_FLOW_DENVER.md): same city, same "put the real stack on one page" purpose, different domain. It uses Denver only because Denver publishes most of the steps in primary sources. The shape of the flow (one address-eligibility question, three permits reviewed together, trade permits that wait on the building permit, and a certificate-of-occupancy gate at the end) is similar in most US cities that allow ADUs; the names and forms are what change.

## Why this page exists

Civic Blueprint's [Problem Map §5](https://github.com/Civic-Blueprint/project-2028/blob/main/PROBLEM_MAP.md#5-housing-is-constrained-where-it-should-be-abundant) says housing is "constrained where it should be abundant," and [Proof-of-Usefulness Memo 01](https://github.com/Civic-Blueprint/project-2028/blob/main/memos/proof-of-usefulness-memo-01.md) argues the binding constraint is often not the _rule_ but the _institution's ability to execute the rule_ — permitting that can actually process the change a city has nominally chosen. An ADU is the smallest clean test of that claim: a homeowner who is legally allowed to add a unit still has to get through a stack of approvals run by agencies that do not share a queue. This page traces that stack for one city so the size and shape of the thing are visible to someone deciding whether to try.

## If you'd rather skim, start here

Three short framings of the same artifact, depending on who is reading. Pick whichever matches the conversation; the rest of the page is the same either way.

**For someone who wants to add an ADU to their own property.** Denver made ADUs legal in far more of the city than before, and that is real. But "allowed" is the first gate, not the last. You still need three permits reviewed together (zoning, building, and a drainage permit), then separate trade permits, then inspections, then a certificate of occupancy before anyone can live in it — and in Denver you generally cannot pull the permits yourself; a licensed contractor has to. This page lays out that sequence.

**For someone who works in housing or permitting more broadly.** An ADU is a clean instance of the execution-capacity problem: the legal reform (Denver's 2023 city-wide ADU zoning changes) is done, but delivery still runs through a multi-agency review whose end-to-end timeline no single reviewer owns. Denver's own response — a 2025 executive order creating a one-stop permitting office with a 180-day cumulative-review target and a fee refund if it misses — is itself a live "demonstrated competence" experiment worth watching. This page traces the flow the reform has to move through.

**For someone who has not seen this kind of thing before.** Adding a small second home in your own backyard in Denver requires, roughly: confirming your lot is allowed to have one, three separate city yes-answers reviewed together, a handful of trade permits, a set of inspections, and a final occupancy sign-off — from offices that each have their own form, fee, and queue. This page lays that out for one specific city so the real shape is legible.

## Step 0: is your lot even allowed to have one?

Everything below assumes the answer is yes. Confirm it first, because if it is no, the rest of the page does not apply until you change it.

- Denver's June 2023 zoning changes made ADUs allowed in more contexts and eased design rules (garage conversions, one-story forms, bulk-plane and lot-coverage treatment). Per the city, ADUs "are allowed in all zone districts that allow new single unit dwellings." ([Denver Adopts New Standards for Accessory Dwelling Units, CPD, 2023](https://denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/CPD-News-and-Events/CPD-News/2023/Denver-Adopts-New-Standards-for-Accessory-Dwelling-Units); [Permitting an Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU)](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits).)
- Check your specific address against the [Denver Zoning Code and zoning maps](https://www.denvergov.org/zoning). If the district does not allow an ADU, the path is a **rezoning** — a formal application with public notice, Planning Board review, and City Council approval, on a multi-month timeline. That is a different and much larger process than the one below.
- Check [floodplain status](https://www.denvergov.org/flood) before anyone drafts plans; a floodplain property can carry constraints that change the design.

## Who you talk to

| Agency | What they decide | Where their public application lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Denver Community Planning and Development (CPD) — Zoning review | Whether the design meets the Denver Zoning Code for an ADU on your lot (size, form, placement). | [ADU Permits](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits) |
| Denver Community Planning and Development (CPD) — Building review | Whether the structure meets building code; issues the residential building permit. | [Denver e-permits](https://www.denvergov.org/epermits) (select "building log") |
| Denver Department of Transportation and Infrastructure (DOTI) — Sewer Use and Drainage Permit (SUDP) team | Whether sanitary, storm/grading, floodplain, groundwater, and right-of-way drainage requirements are met. | [Sewer Use and Drainage Permits (SUDP)](https://denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Department-of-Transportation-and-Infrastructure/Documents/Wastewater-Permits/Sewer-Use-and-Drainage-Permits) |
| Denver CPD — Trade permit review (electrical, plumbing, mechanical, roofing) | Whether each trade's work meets code. Usually issued online as "quick permits." | [ADU Permits — trade permits](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits) |
| Denver Landmark Preservation (only if applicable) | Whether the design is approved when the property is a landmark or in a historic district — this comes _before_ the permits above. | [Landmark Design Review](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Landmark-Preservation/Landmark-Reviews-and-Demolition/Landmark-Design-Review) |

Two layers of review sit inside one online submission: CPD (zoning + building) and DOTI (drainage). Trade permits and, where relevant, Landmark are separate tracks around them.

## The order of operations

Some of these run together. Others cannot start until something else has finished. The trap is in the second column.

| Can run together | Must wait on something else |
| --- | --- |
| Apply for zoning, building, and SUDP at the same time. Per the city, projects that "upload all of the information required for these three permit types (zoning, building and SUDP) with their initial online application can receive a simultaneous review." | If the property is a landmark or in a historic district, Landmark Preservation approval must be obtained **first** — the permits above will not clear without it. |
| Prepare the plan set once (architectural, structural, site plan showing water/storm/sanitary lines) since the same set feeds all three reviews. | Trade permits for new construction. **Since May 22, 2025, a trade permit for new construction requires an issued residential (building) permit before it can proceed** — so plumbing/electrical/mechanical cannot start until the building permit issues. |
| Line up a licensed contractor early (see the friction section — in Denver you generally cannot pull ADU permits yourself). | Inspections are scheduled only after the relevant permit is issued and the work it covers is ready to look at. |
| Check floodplain status and confirm ADU zoning eligibility before drafting. | A **Certificate of Occupancy is required before the ADU can be lived in** — it is the last gate, and it depends on every inspection above having passed. |

## Where most people actually get stuck

Four friction points show up repeatedly in the published process — not because any one agency is slow, but because of how the dependencies stack and who is allowed to act.

1. **You usually cannot pull the permits yourself.** Denver's homeowner-permit path explicitly excludes ADUs: "A homeowner cannot obtain a permit for work in accessory dwelling units (ADUs), townhomes, condos, or duplexes, even for the unit where you live." In practice that means engaging a licensed contractor (and, for the plan set, licensed design professionals) before the process can even begin — a cost and a gate that surprises owners who assumed a backyard unit was a do-it-yourself project. Source: [Applying for Permits as a Homeowner](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/Homeowner-Permits).

2. **"Simultaneous review" only works if the first submission is complete.** The three-permit review runs together **only** when zoning, building, and SUDP information is all uploaded with the initial application. A partial submission drops you into sequential review and resubmittal rounds instead. The incentive is to front-load a complete, coordinated plan set — which is exactly the step an inexperienced applicant is least equipped to do. Source: [ADU Permits](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits).

3. **Trade permits wait on the building permit; occupancy waits on everything.** Because trade permits for new construction now require an issued residential permit (effective May 22, 2025), the plumbing and electrical work cannot begin the moment you break ground — it waits on the building permit clearing review. And the Certificate of Occupancy, without which no one can move in, waits on every inspection passing. A delay early in the building review propagates all the way to move-in. Source: [ADU Permits](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits).

4. **The published review target and the real end-to-end timeline are different numbers.** Denver's own policy sets an initial residential plan-review target of **10 business days** ([ADMIN 131A](https://denver.prelive.opencities.com/files/assets/public/v/6/community-planning-and-development/documents/ds/building-codes/policies/admin-131a.pdf)). But that is one review task, not the whole journey: each resubmittal round resets a new review, and the drainage and trade tracks run their own clocks. Before the 2025 reform, the city's public dashboard showed approvals averaging **355 days for major residential projects** ([Denver Post, April 14, 2025](https://www.denverpost.com/2025/04/14/denver-construction-building-permits-process-mike-johnston/)). The gap between the per-task target and the lived timeline is the friction — and it is precisely what Denver's 2025 permitting reform is trying to close (see below).

The pattern is the same one the [childcare-licensing flow](CHILDCARE_LICENSING_FLOW_DENVER.md) shows: every late-stage approval depends on something earlier finishing in a different track that does not see the rest of the queue.

## Honest timing and cost (Denver, as published)

Numbers below are pulled from the agencies' own published materials, or from named reporting where noted. They are targets and averages, not guarantees for your project.

- **Initial residential plan-review target:** **10 business days** for the initial review, 10 for each resubmittal, 2 for intake — per [ADMIN 131A, Plan Review Target Review Dates](https://denver.prelive.opencities.com/files/assets/public/v/6/community-planning-and-development/documents/ds/building-codes/policies/admin-131a.pdf). New ADUs are classified as a "Residential" building log.
- **End-to-end city review cap (2025 reform):** Executive Order 151 (May 2025) created the Denver Permitting Office with a target of **180 days of cumulative city review time** (excluding time waiting on the applicant). If the city misses it and a review board cannot decide within 30 days, the applicant "may be able to recoup up to $10,000 in application fees." ([Denver Post, April 14, 2025](https://www.denverpost.com/2025/04/14/denver-construction-building-permits-process-mike-johnston/); [How the 180 days is measured, DPO procedure](https://denver.prelive.opencities.com/files/assets/public/v/2/permitting/documents/how-180-days-measured-procedure-250604.pdf).)
- **Pre-reform baseline (for contrast):** the city's dashboard showed approvals averaging **355 days for major residential** and **256 days for major commercial** projects before the reform. ([Denver Post, April 14, 2025](https://www.denverpost.com/2025/04/14/denver-construction-building-permits-process-mike-johnston/).)
- **Permits required:** zoning, building, and SUDP for the structure; plus separate trade permits (electrical, plumbing, mechanical, roofing) issued in most cases as online "quick permits." ([ADU Permits](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Single-Family-and-Duplex-Projects/ADU-Permits).)
- **Affordable Housing Linkage Fee:** applies to new construction and "must be paid at the time of building permit issuance." ([Residential Permitting Guide](https://www.denvergov.org/files/assets/public/v/2/doti/documents/permits/sudp/permittingguide_residentialsfduplex.pdf).)
- **Fees generally:** building, zoning, and DOTI/SUDP fees are set on published schedules at [denvergov.org/DSfees](https://www.denvergov.org/DSfees); exact amounts scale with valuation and scope, so this page does not quote a single figure.

## Friction patterns this one-pager does not show on its own

The flow above tells you _who decides what_, in _what order_. Practitioners who have run a residential build-out — especially in a historic-district context, but not only there — consistently report additional patterns that the per-agency view does not surface. They are the same structural patterns the [childcare-licensing flow](CHILDCARE_LICENSING_FLOW_DENVER.md#friction-patterns-this-one-pager-does-not-show-on-its-own) documents, and they are not Denver-specific in their structure; the Denver references here are illustrative.

1. **Scope-creep at inspection vs. approved plans.** Plans can pass plan review and still pick up new required items at field inspection — life-safety, egress, and fire-separation items that were not on the approved set but become the binding requirement once an inspector is on site. A budget set against approved plans can reopen during inspection. ([Denver Regulations, Codes and Standards](https://www.denvergov.org/My-Property/Remodeling-and-Construction/Permit-Office/Code).)

2. **Multi-track approval inconsistency — informal "yes" overridden by formal "no."** A courtesy or pre-application opinion from one office does not bind the office that actually issues the permit. This shows up most sharply where Landmark or zoning staff give early encouragement that a downstream permit reviewer later rejects. The right question when you hear "yes" is _is this the office that issues the permit?_ — not _is this office in agreement?_ ([Landmark Design Review](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Landmark-Preservation/Landmark-Reviews-and-Demolition/Landmark-Design-Review).)

3. **Historic-district / Landmark review as a rate-limiter on everything else.** When the property is a landmark or in a historic district, a separate design-review track runs in parallel with — and gates — the permit flow. The rate-limiter is meeting cadence: review bodies meet on fixed schedules and need quorum to act, so a missed quorum or a deferred item can add weeks or more, regardless of how fast the permit reviewers move. Most Denver ADUs are not in landmarked properties; for the ones that are, this schedule can become the dominant constraint. ([Landmark Design Review](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Landmark-Preservation/Landmark-Reviews-and-Demolition/Landmark-Design-Review).)

4. **Targets vs. lived timeline.** As the timing section notes, a 10-business-day per-task target and a pre-reform ~355-day average end-to-end are both true at once, because the journey is many tasks with resubmittal rounds and parallel tracks. This is the institutional-capacity gap [Memo 01](https://github.com/Civic-Blueprint/project-2028/blob/main/memos/proof-of-usefulness-memo-01.md) names: the rule allowing ADUs is not the same as the state's ability to deliver them on a timeline a homeowner can plan against. Denver's 2025 permitting reform is a direct, testable attempt to close it — the kind of "demonstrated competence" move [Problem Map §4](https://github.com/Civic-Blueprint/project-2028/blob/main/PROBLEM_MAP.md#4-institutional-capacity-is-too-weak-for-the-demands-placed-on-it) treats as an entry point for rebuilding trust.

These patterns do not change the order of operations above; they change the budget envelope and the calendar around it. Add a contingency for each pattern that applies to your specific project — most acutely if the property is landmarked or in a historic district.

## What is _not_ in this one-pager

This page deliberately leaves out:

- The full Denver Zoning Code ADU rules (maximum size, height, bulk plane, form standards by district). Those are real and load-bearing but live in the [Denver Zoning Code](https://www.denvergov.org/zoning), not in a one-page flow.
- Rezoning, if your lot does not already allow an ADU. That is a separate, larger process (application, public notice, Planning Board, City Council).
- Financing an ADU (construction loans, HELOCs, the economics of rent vs. cost). Different track entirely.
- Detailed fee amounts. These scale with valuation and scope; see [denvergov.org/DSfees](https://www.denvergov.org/DSfees).
- Utility service upgrades (water tap sizing, electrical service capacity) where a specific site needs them.
- The rules for renting the finished unit (short-term vs. long-term rental licensing).
